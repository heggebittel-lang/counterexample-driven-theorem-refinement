# Exposition audit: make the research operation legible

This is an optional **communication pass** after the mathematics has been
checked. It is not a substitute for proof verification. Use it when preparing
a personal essay, research note, preprint, talk, or public prompt.

## 1. Replace slogans with executable operations

A first-time reader should know what the instruction tells the researcher to
do. Replace:

> Delete assumptions.

with something like:

> Remove one stated assumption at a time. Try to prove the same conclusion
> from the remaining assumptions; if the attempt fails, seek a genuine
> counterexample satisfying all of them.

A verb is only clear if its input, action, and possible outputs can be
identified.

## 2. Give mathematical symbols an unambiguous meaning

Do not write an unexplained arrow such as `AB -> CD -> E` and assume the
reader knows whether juxtaposition means conjunction, a lemma, or a sequence.

Prefer a concrete dependency example:

- continuity plus opposite endpoint signs -> existence of a zero;
- strict monotonicity -> at most one zero;
- both -> a unique zero.

If the notation remains abstract, define every object and the intended
logical connection.

## 3. Do not let illustrative diagrams become false theorems

From `C => P` alone, `not C => Q` does **not** follow.

If a theory seems to have two regimes, verify both implications separately,
then test whether the regimes are disjoint and exhaustive. If not proved,
call it a *desired characterization*, not an established partition.

Likewise, distinguish:

- "no guarantee of uniqueness" (counterexamples exist);
- "nonuniqueness is inevitable" (every admissible case has multiple solutions);
- "strict convexity implies at most one minimizer" (a sufficient uniqueness
  condition, with existence as a separate question).

## 4. Give every paragraph one job

For reflective posts, a useful sequence is:

1. Who is speaking and the question (one short paragraph).
2. The concrete method, with brief local explanations.
3. One real research episode explaining how the method arose.
4. What AI does in that episode and what still needs verification.
5. One or two questions for readers.

Do not repeatedly introduce the author's background, insist that the method
is useful, or restate the same sequence of steps after giving the numbered list.

## 5. Separate research writing from personal narrative

In a formal theorem write definitions, conditions, and conclusions rather
than the conversational history of how they were found.

In a first-person community essay, however, keep a brief and honest research
story. A personal statement is useful when it supplies an example or explains
the author's question; it is redundant when it merely repeats the author's
lack of certainty or institutional position.

## 6. Compactness without flattening voice

Do not convert a researcher's distinctive experience into generic
promotional or corporate prose. Compress repeated framing, not the specific
episode that explains the insight.

Preserve:
- the interesting counterexample and what was learned from it;
- concrete operations rather than "explore, think, improve";
- uncertainty about claims not yet validated externally;
- the user's natural and accessible voice.

Remove:
- generic praise;
- long meta-introductions;
- unexplained symbolic shorthand;
- multiple restatements of the same point;
- claims that an editor's interest validates theorem correctness.

## Final checklist

- Can a new reader execute the first listed step?
- Does each symbol have a defined meaning?
- Are sufficient and necessary claims distinguished?
- Are complementary cases genuinely proved?
- Is a counterexample absorbed only when the new claim is established?
- Does each paragraph add something new?
- Is the personal background stated just once?
- Does the last paragraph ask a precise question rather than repeat the post?
- Is translation/editing assistance disclosed accurately?
